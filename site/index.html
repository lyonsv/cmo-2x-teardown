01

Claim & measurement

The headline metric counts approved-and-live asset-variants per marketing head per quarter. The denominator is all marketing FTE: brand, operations, regional and agency-equivalent staff. Assets that are generated but not approved never count. The number is always shown beside unique concepts and variants per concept, so growth from new ideas cannot hide behind growth from cheap copies.

The count is of approved-and-live variants only. To reach it the model derives more variants than it publishes, at an approval yield of 95% Modelled; the next section costs the rejected ones.

What 2x does not mean

It does not mean twice as many assets generated, twice the budget, or half the team. It means twice as many approved-and-live asset-variants per marketing head per quarter, within a flat total marketing budget, with outcomes that hold.

Falsifier

If approved-live variants per head double but holdout-measured conversion/CAC does not improve, or first-pass compliance falls, within 2–3 quarters, the 2x claim is false for that org.

02

The CFO answer

Budget: flat in total, not in production

Gartner reports marketing budgets at 7.7% Benchmark of company revenue in its earlier survey and 7.8% Benchmark in its latest, from a self-reported survey of 402 CMOs in North America, the UK and Europe, mostly at large companies. That is an average across respondents, not a rule for any one organisation. The consequence is the same either way: budgets are not rising to meet the ambition, so 2x has to come from what each approved asset costs, not from new money.

Cost per approved, live asset

Fully loaded, an approved, live asset falls from $64.71 Modelled to $41.18, a change of −36%. The cost is built from six components: generation, review, rework, tooling, rights and storage. It includes the cost of variants that were derived and reviewed but rejected at the gate, at the approval yield of 95% Modelled. Rejected variants are costed but never counted as output.

The scope is production-attributable cost only: $2.59M Modelled a year today and $3.29M at 2x, or 3.3% Modelled and 4.2% of the marketing budget. It excludes media and non-production headcount.

Production cost therefore rises by $0.71M Modelled a year, a change of +27%, or 0.9 points Modelled of the marketing budget. Cost per asset falls, but output doubles, so the total still goes up. The total marketing budget stays flat only because that rise is reallocated from other marketing lines, chiefly agency and production fees Modelled. If those lines cannot shrink, the budget is not flat and the claim has to be restated.

Production is a small line in the budget, so the per-asset saving matters less than what the extra approved output does for outcomes, which the outcome case section takes up. The figure below breaks the cost down by component.

Review is the binding constraint

The model holds at flat review headcount only if at least 41% Modelled of variants clear with no human touch, which keeps reviewers at or below the 85% Modelled utilisation target. The page assumes 20% today, 60% at 2x. Below the break-even share, queues and cycle time grow quickly. Below about 29%, review capacity is exceeded outright and the 2x programme stalls in the queue, whatever the cost per asset says.

AI spend

AI spend rises from $38,421 Modelled to $340,421 a year, a multiple of 8.9x. That is the price of the substitution, not a target to hit.

AI spend framing

AI spend is a floor, not a target. Teams that are rewarded for spending on AI will spend on AI (Goodhart's law); the measure that matters is the cost of an approved, live asset.

03

The factory model

A marketing organisation scales by deriving variants from a few human-directed concepts, not by asking a model for ten thousand originals.

Think of the marketing organisation as a factory rather than a studio. A factory has inputs, stages and queues, and it is judged on throughput, defect rate and cost per good unit. The inputs here are briefs and a small set of master concepts. The stages are derivation, review and publishing. Queues form wherever one stage produces faster than the next can absorb. Seen this way, doubling output is a question about the slowest stage, not about how quickly anyone can draft copy.

The principle is to derive, not generate. A few master concepts carry the creative risk: the idea, the claim, the look and the offer. People direct and approve them, and that is the slow, expensive part by design. Most of the variants that follow are mechanical derivations: a template applied, a locale swapped, a feed rendered at the sizes a channel demands. A derivation changes the form of an approved idea; it does not invent a new one. That is how variants multiply without multiplying risk.

The review stage is where the constraint moves. Generation and derivation are cheap and scale with compute. Review does not, because every variant needs a decision against the same rules. Once derivation is automated, reviewer capacity sets the pace, and a programme that ignores this only builds a longer queue in front of the same few people. That is why review is drawn as the gate, and why the auto-approval gate is treated in detail later.

The content supply chain is the full path from brief to measurement and back. A brief states audience, offer and goal. A concept answers it. Derivation multiplies the concept into the variants a campaign needs, review checks each one against written policy, and publishing sends approved variants to channels.

The return path matters most. Measurement turns the line into a loop: what performed, in which market and for which audience, becomes input to the next brief. The thread that holds the stages together is the asset manifest, a record that gives every variant an identity and carries its concept, policy version, review findings and results. Without it the loop cannot close, because performance cannot be traced to the decision that produced it. The platform layers show where the manifest sits.

One caution, in general terms. Output counted before quality is measured can look like progress while outcomes dip, so measure quality before raising volume, and keep measuring after.

Platform and skills

One versioned policy source, read by both the generator and the reviewer, is what makes the layers of a marketing factory governable.

The table reads from intent at the top to measurement at the bottom, and it names tool categories rather than products on purpose. Any layer can be filled by several kinds of tool, and the architecture holds if one is swapped for another. What does not change is the order of responsibility: intent is captured, assets are built and derived, they are reviewed, a gate decides who approves, and results are measured against the same record.

The first row carries the weight. Brand, legal, accessibility and claims rules live in one versioned policy source, and both the generator and the reviewer read the same version of it. If they read different copies, the reviewer ends up checking work against rules the generator never saw, and disagreements look like defects when they are really drift. Every finding therefore records the policy version it was checked against. A change to policy is a versioned release, with an owner and a date, not an edit to a prompt that nobody tracks.

Skills are the second idea. A skill is a reusable unit of work, such as localising a headline, rendering a feed or checking a claim, packaged with its instructions and its own tests. Treating skills as software changes how they are managed. Each has a version, an owner and a set of examples that must keep passing, and a change to a skill is released only after it has been checked against those examples. The discipline is simple to state: measure, build, enforce, re-measure.

Skills also keep people in the right place. People write the policy and direct the concepts; skills carry out the repeatable work; the review agent applies policy to every variant; humans take the decisions the gate sends them. Each of these roles can be inspected, because each leaves a record.

The asset manifest is the join key that makes this inspection possible. It is the system of record for every variant, and every layer reads from it or writes to it. A variant, its concept, its policy version, its review findings, its approval and its performance are all attached to one identifier. That identifier is used again when eval harness and telemetry compares the reviewer with human decisions, and it is what allows a programme to say which rule, which skill and which policy version produced a given result.

05

The 10,000-asset campaign

Derive, do not generate: four master concepts become about 12,000 Modelled variants through templating, localisation and feed rendering, and most land in low-risk tiers.

A campaign of this size is not written one asset at a time. People brief it and approve a handful of master concepts. Everything after that is a derivation, and each derivation lands in the asset manifest before anyone judges it.

Most variants are mechanical derivations of approved masters, which is why most land in low-risk tiers and reach the auto-approval gate with a small chance of sending anything to a person.

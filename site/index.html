What we mean by 2x

Doubling output is easy to claim and easy to fake. This page counts one thing: useful work completed per marketing FTE-equivalent, with agency FTE-equivalents in the denominator so that moving work to an agency cannot fake a gain. The other measures guard it, or sit beside it as outcomes.

Capacity, efficiency and effectiveness

Capacity
How much useful work the same people complete. The primary metric measures this.
Efficiency
What the same work costs, after agency fees, software and platform cost. Hours released are cash only when they are removable.
Effectiveness
Whether the extra work improves business outcomes. This is not known until it is measured.

Doubled capacity does not establish doubled revenue. Large studies of advertising find small average effects and sharply diminishing returns, and field experiments are too noisy to settle returns from a single campaign. So the page treats capacity as the claim and outcomes as hypotheses with named tests. What it means for CMO outcomes sets out the four mechanisms that could carry a real gain.

Why marketing has no merged pull request

Software teams have a clean unit of completed work: the merged pull request. It sits in one system of record, has a clear end state, is checked by tests, and reaches customers within minutes. Marketing has none of these.

  • There is no single system of record or merge event. Work is spread across planning tools, asset stores, publishing and advertising platforms and agency trackers.
  • Published work cannot be reverted. An advert that has been seen stays seen.
  • Ground truth is contested. There is no test suite for persuasion, and brand harm is diffuse.
  • Outcomes lag by weeks or quarters, and credit for pipeline is shared with sales, product and partners.

So the unit has to be constructed. A request closes only when the system verifies the terminal event: it is live in its channel, or its owner has accepted the research or plan. Each unit has a size class set at intake by a rubric, not by the requester, and baseline hours that are frozen. Quality comes from pre-flight checks and sampled human audit rather than from reverts. Outcomes are tracked separately, as lagging results to test, not as the productivity measure.

Where the evidence stops. The definition of the target is a choice, made so a CFO can check it. The multiples shown are outputs of this page's model, not observations of any organisation, and nothing here shows outcomes improving until a pilot measures them.

What 2x does not mean

It does not mean twice as many assets generated, twice the budget, or half the team. It means twice the useful work completed per marketing FTE-equivalent, the primary metric defined in What we mean by 2x, with quality guardrails that hold and outcomes measured on their own. For the first workflow the operational unit is campaign-markets launched per quarter, and extra crops, sizes or locales of the same campaign-market do not count.

In the first-edition launch-rate scenario, flat marketing headcount is assumed, not tested Speculative. That scenario checks reviewer capacity only, not whether marketers can make the extra master concepts and run the extra launches.

Falsifier

If useful work per marketing FTE-equivalent doubles but holdout-measured conversion/CAC does not improve, or first-pass compliance falls, within 2–3 quarters, the 2x claim is false for that org. For the first workflow the same test applies to campaign-markets launched per quarter.

The CFO answer

Budget: flat in total, not in production

Gartner reports marketing budgets at 7.7% Benchmark of company revenue in its earlier survey and 7.8% Benchmark in its latest, from a self-reported survey of 402 CMOs in North America, the UK and Europe, mostly at large companies. That is an average across respondents, not a rule for any one organisation. The consequence is the same either way: budgets have flattened, so 2x has to come from what each approved asset and each campaign-market launched costs, not from new money.

Cost per approved asset

At activity cost (internal hours at one price per hour plus external spend), an approved asset falls from $152.18 Modelled to $106.38, a change of −30%. Each master concept carries 35.2 hours Modelled of internal time as well as the agency fee. The cost is built from six components: generation, review, rework, tooling, rights and storage. It includes the cost of variants that were derived and reviewed but rejected at the gate, at the approval yield of 95% Modelled. Rejected variants are costed but never counted as output.

Cost per campaign-market

Production cost per campaign-market falls from $30,436 Modelled to $21,276, a change of −30%. In this model every campaign-market carries the same derived variants, so it moves with cost per approved asset. Doubling through more markets reuses master concepts and costs less per asset than doubling through more campaigns, which need new concepts; the calculator lets you choose the route.

The scope is production-attributable cost only: $6.09M Modelled a year today and $8.51M at 2x, or 7.8% Modelled and 10.9% of the marketing budget. It excludes media and non-production headcount.

Production cost therefore rises by $2.42M Modelled a year, a change of +40%, or 3.1 points Modelled of the marketing budget. Cost per approved asset falls, but campaign-markets launched per quarter double, and the default route adds master concepts, so the total still goes up. The total marketing budget stays flat only because that rise is assumed to come from other marketing lines; agency spend is modelled as its own line Modelled. If those lines cannot shrink, the budget is not flat and the claim has to be restated.

Even so, the per-asset saving matters less than what the extra campaign launches do for outcomes, which the outcome case section takes up. The figure below breaks the cost down by component.

Review is the binding constraint

The model holds at flat review headcount only if at least 41% Modelled of variants are auto-approved by the gate, which keeps reviewers at or below the 85% Modelled utilisation target. The page assumes 20% today, 60% at 2x. Below the break-even share, queues and brief-to-live time grow quickly. Below about 29%, review capacity is exceeded outright and the 2x programme stalls in the queue, whatever the cost per asset says.

AI spend

AI spend rises from $38,421 Modelled to $340,421 a year, a multiple of 8.9x. That is the price of the substitution, not a target to hit.

AI spend framing

AI spend is a floor, not a target. Teams that are rewarded for spending on AI will spend on AI (Goodhart's law); the measure that matters is the cost of an approved, live asset.

Why hand agents whole workflows?

Software built → Autonomy enabled → Human work reduced → Marketing capacity increased → Business outcomes improved

Would giving agents responsibility for whole workflows, rather than asking them for more content, raise what a marketing team can complete? This section argues that it would, sets out the one published programme that did something like this, and says where that evidence stops.

The argument is short. More generated content does not double what a team finishes if the constraint sits in review, publishing and measurement. Faster drafting only moves the queue. The gain comes from removing handoffs, and that means agents taking responsibility for whole stages of the work, inside limits that people set and can inspect.

The autonomy ladder

Autonomy comes in steps. Each step hands the agent more of the work and gives people a different job.

Four levels of autonomy
LevelWhat the agent doesWhat people doJustified whenNot justified when
Assistance Suggests, drafts or answers when asked, one step at a time. Decide, direct and do the work. The agent speeds up single tasks. The task is new, unclear or judged case by case, or no checks exist yet. The work is repeatable and checkable. Staying here leaves the waiting and handoffs between steps untouched.
Agentic execution Carries out a defined task of several steps, using tools, and hands the result back. Set the task and review the result. Inputs are clear, a good result is known, and checks run automatically. The result cannot be checked cheaply, or a mistake would reach customers before anyone looks.
Higher autonomy Runs a whole workflow across its stages, acts within set permissions and stops where a rule says to. Set the rules and limits, handle exceptions and sample the output. The work is low risk or can be withdrawn, the checks have been tested against human decisions, and every action is logged. A wrong output cannot be withdrawn, or reviewers themselves disagree about what is correct.
Outcome-directed autonomy Is given a business objective, plans the work, runs it, measures the result and proposes changes. Own the objective, the limits and the spend, and approve changes of direction. Only after the lower levels have measured results, and the outcome can be tied to the work. Outcomes arrive late and credit is shared, so an agent could optimise the wrong signal at speed.

Higher autonomy is not automatically better. A step up is justified by reliability, economic value, risk and measured outcomes, in that order of proof, and every step has a place where people can pull the cord. The software described in section 03 is what makes each step inspectable, and the first workflow in section 04 is where this page proposes starting.

What Intercom measured, and why

Intercom, a software company, told its research and development staff in public that it wanted to double output in a year. It measured progress as merged pull requests divided by the number of people in R&D. A pull request is a proposed code change, and merged means accepted into the product. src-fin-2x-2025

The chief technology officer gave three reasons for dividing by everyone, managers and designers included. That is where all the cost sits, everyone can affect the number directly or indirectly, and it puts pressure on how the organisation is designed. He also used the number to find bottlenecks. Raising the expectation exposed whatever slowed the whole system down, and the team then fixed it. src-fin-2x-nine-months

He was open about the weak points. Pull requests differ in size and value, the measure is easy to criticise, and it does not translate directly into business impact: in his words, it is "required, but not sufficient". The definition also drifted, from pull requests per engineer in the first post to pull requests per person in R&D in the later ones. This page uses the later one. src-fin-webinar-qa

Demonstrated and uncertain

Demonstrated, with the caveat that all of it is self-reported

  • A large and fast change in behaviour. Most pull requests became agent-driven within a few months.
  • Output per person rose by about a factor of two on Intercom's own definition. Several indicators moved together: pull requests, deployments, features shipped and time from idea to release. None of them is independent of Intercom.
  • No visible collapse in quality on the measures Intercom tracks. The defect backlog and attributable downtime both fell.
  • Review automation worked for a narrow, low-risk class of change: mostly small ones, merged faster, with no reverts in a pilot of around a hundred changes. src-intercom-ai-approving-prs
  • The constraint moved from writing code to testing to review.

Uncertain or not demonstrated

  • Nothing is audited. Absolute counts, the baseline month and cost per change are not published.
  • There is no counterfactual: no control group and no comparison with other companies.
  • Better underlying models arrived during the same months, and the data cannot separate that from the programme's own changes.
  • The longer threefold claim covers a window that starts before the goal was announced.
  • Business outcomes are asserted, not measured. The company says revenue is a lagging indicator and publishes no revenue figure tied to the programme. src-fin-webinar-qa
  • The pull-request count can be raised by making changes smaller, and the approval rule rewards small changes.
  • The average hides a long tail: a few heavy users produce several times the median. src-fin-2x-nine-months
  • Intercom says its culture and systems are hard to copy. src-fin-webinar-qa

The bottleneck moved

Intercom's account runs in a sequence. Writing code became fast, which strained the build and test pipeline. Once that was fixed, human review of pull requests became the queue, so the team built an AI reviewer with a risk-gated approval path. Next in line, by its own account, is diagnosing problems in production, which is still unsolved. src-fin-2x-nine-months

The same thing would happen in marketing. This is our reasoning, not Intercom's evidence.

What does not transfer

  • Publication cannot be reversed. A revert is cheap in software and an advertisement cannot be unseen, so the risk tiers must be more conservative and monitoring after launch stands in for revert.
  • Ground truth is contested. Reviewers disagree about what is on brand or compliant, so there is no clean answer key against which to measure an agent.
  • There is no merge event and no single system of record, so the unit of work has to be constructed and instrumented.
  • Outcomes arrive late and credit is shared with sales and product, so early proof rests on capacity, speed and quality, with outcome tests designed in from the start.
  • Labour is a minority of marketing spend, so a gain per person moves the total budget less than it did in engineering. Agencies sit outside headcount and can make per-head figures look better without any change in output.
  • Intercom already had a shipping culture and automated testing and release. Most marketing organisations lack the equivalent: preflight validation and verified publishing. It is a build item, and it is the main reason a marketing 2x would take longer.

What needs to be built

Software built → Autonomy enabled → Human work reduced → Marketing capacity increased → Business outcomes improved

What exactly would we build, and what is the smallest version that proves it? This section describes the system in plain terms, lists the ten capabilities it needs and says which to build, which to integrate and which existing platforms already provide.

The system in plain English

A marketer gives the system an objective, such as launching an approved campaign in more markets by a set date, within a budget, using only approved claims. The system plans the work and does it in the tools the team already uses. It checks its own output against the brand, legal and quality rules, and sends people only the decisions that exceed its permissions or its confidence. It confirms that the work went live correctly, then reports whether the objective was met.

Campaign owners set objectives and limits and approve plans. Brand, legal and regional reviewers decide exceptions, not routine work. A small operations and platform team owns the rules, the permissions, the connections and the tests.

It is not a new content generator, a replacement for the marketing suite, or a platform that must be finished before anything works. The first version runs one workflow from start to finish, and later workflows reuse the same parts.

Six parts

The system has six parts. The ten capabilities below are these parts broken into the pieces someone has to build or buy.

  1. An objective record for each piece of work: goal, measures, limits, owner and deadline. It covers the objective record capability.
  2. A plan, drawn from a tested template for that workflow, filled in by an agent and approved by a person. It covers planning and orchestration.
  3. Agents that act through controlled connections to the systems the team already uses, each connection carrying explicit permissions. It covers connectors and permissions, and worker agents and skills.
  4. Automatic checks on every output: rule checks first, then a separate review agent, then sampling by people. It covers the library and the checks and evaluations.
  5. An approval inbox where exceptions arrive with their evidence attached. It covers the approval inbox.
  6. A run record and outcome measures that log every step, decision and result against the objective and feed the next plan. It covers the run record, measurement, and feedback and optimisation.

The table reads from intent at the top to measurement at the bottom, and it names tool categories rather than products on purpose. Any layer can be filled by several kinds of tool, and the architecture holds if one is swapped for another. What does not change is the order of responsibility: intent is captured, assets are built and derived, they are reviewed, a gate decides who approves, and results are measured against the same record.

The first row carries the weight. Brand, legal, accessibility and claims rules live in one versioned policy source, and both the generator and the reviewer read the same version of it. If they read different copies, the reviewer ends up checking work against rules the generator never saw, and disagreements look like defects when they are really drift. Every finding therefore records the policy version it was checked against. A change to policy is a versioned release, with an owner and a date, not an edit to a prompt that nobody tracks.

Skills are the second idea. A skill is a reusable unit of work, such as localising a headline, rendering a feed or checking a claim, packaged with its instructions and its own tests. Treating skills as software changes how they are managed. Each has a version, an owner and a set of examples that must keep passing, and a change to a skill is released only after it has been checked against those examples. The discipline is simple to state: measure, build, enforce, re-measure.

Skills also keep people in the right place. People write the policy and direct the concepts; skills carry out the repeatable work; the review agent applies policy to every variant; humans take the decisions the gate sends them. Each of these roles can be inspected, because each leaves a record.

The asset manifest is the join key that makes this inspection possible. It is the system of record for every variant, and every layer reads from it or writes to it. A variant, its concept, its policy version, its review findings, its approval and its performance are all attached to one identifier. That identifier is used again when eval harness and telemetry compares the reviewer with human decisions, and it is what allows a programme to say which rule, which skill and which policy version produced a given result.

Worked example: launch the approved campaign in three additional markets

Take a campaign approved and running in one market. The owner wants it live in three more by a set date. This is the pilot described in the roadmap, run across the pilot workflows. Each step below is marked Agent or People.

  1. People The owner sets the objective: launch in markets A, B and C by the date, on paid social and a landing page, within a budget cap for each market, using only claims approved for those markets. The measure is qualified visits or leads against the source market's first two weeks. Complaint and disapproval rates are the guardrails, and the permission level is stage. This is one decision.
  2. Agent drafts the plan from the market-launch template: the masters needed, the variants for each channel and market, the rules that apply, linguist sample sizes, dates, and what it cannot do. For example: "market C needs a disclosure that is not in the approved claims library, so one item needs legal." People then approve the plan.
  3. Agent pulls masters and rights records from the asset manager. One image's licence excludes market B, so it substitutes an approved alternative of the same risk tier and records why. This removes a rights chase that today costs several emails.
  4. Agent derives the sizes and channel cuts and localises the copy using the glossary and translation memory. Low-scoring segments go to a linguist. The rest go to a linguist sample.
  5. Agent runs rule checks on specs, links, tracking parameters and disclosures, and the review agent checks claims, brand and rights for each market, citing the rule it applied. Fix loops are bounded. Two kinds of item cross the approval boundary: the market C disclosure, and anything the agent could not check. Everything else is batched with its evidence attached.
  6. People decide. Legal approves or rewrites the disclosure, and the new wording enters the claims library for next time. Each regional lead approves their market as a batch from a sample view, not asset by asset.
  7. Agent builds ads in a paused state and pages in preview, then reads them back from each platform: correct targeting, correct asset identifiers, landing pages that resolve, tracking that fires, and a budget equal to the cap.
  8. People click go-live, once for each market. A later step on the expansion path makes this automatic for markets already approved.
  9. Agent compares each market with the source-market benchmark and the guardrails at the fixed early-read point, and writes a short report to the run record.
  10. Agent proposes what to do next. For example: "variant X is fatiguing in market B, so refresh it with the approved alternate headline; the market C landing page has a high bounce rate on mobile, so open a test." People decide. A later step applies the low-risk refresh automatically.

Why removing hand-offs beats faster generation

Faster generation shortens one step. A hand-off is a wait, a ticket, a briefing and often a second review. In the worked example the agent does not mainly save time by writing faster. It removes the tickets, the separate briefings for each supplier, the agency and vendor round trips, the repeated whole-batch reviews, the trafficking requests and the report requests that sit between the steps.

One run with a few human decisions replaces a chain of them. That is why the model counts hand-offs and waiting time as well as production minutes, and why the days elapsed above move as well as the hours.

Generation alone also moves the bottleneck. More assets with the same reviews only build a longer queue in front of the same few people. The split between what scale delivers and what a redesigned programme delivers is in the economics.

The factory model

A marketing organisation scales by deriving variants from a few human-directed concepts, not by asking a model for ten thousand originals.

The principle is to derive, not generate. A few master concepts carry the creative risk: the idea, the claim, the look and the offer. People direct and approve them. In the adapt stage, most variants are mechanical derivations: a template applied, a locale swapped, a feed rendered at the sizes a channel demands. A derivation changes the form of an approved idea; it does not invent a new one. That is how variants multiply without multiplying risk. The factory model in full is in Appendix A.

The review stage is where the constraint moves. Generation and derivation are cheap and scale with compute. Review does not, because every variant needs a decision against the same rules. Once derivation is automated, reviewer capacity sets the pace, and a programme that ignores this only builds a longer queue. That is why review is a gate in the diagram, and why the auto-approval gate is treated in detail later.

The return path matters most. Measurement turns the content supply chain into a loop: what performed, in which market and for which audience, becomes input to the next plan. The run record holds the stages together. It gives every variant an identity and carries its objective, policy version, review findings and results. Without it the loop cannot close, because performance cannot be traced to the decision that produced it. The platform layers show where the run record sits.

One caution, in general terms. Output counted before quality is measured can look like progress while outcomes dip, so measure quality before raising volume, and keep measuring after.

Three ways to use the capacity

Released capacity can be used in three different ways, and each has a different financial meaning. The model keeps them apart so that none of them is counted as another. Scenario C is the default.

A. Same team, more output. The in-house team stays fixed and the released capacity makes more output. That output also needs agency work, so agency spend grows with it. There is no cash saving: the net cash effect is −$7,481,083 Speculative, which is the run cost of the programme plus the extra agency spend shown in the table. It earns a return only if the extra output earns one, and that is the question for the outcomes section.

B. Same output, lower cost. Output stays at today's level and the saving is taken in cost. The removable headcount of 39.0 Speculative FTE is a measure of capacity that could be released, not a recommendation to remove anyone. Capacity spread thinly across many people is not removable, and the severance estimate is Speculative.

C. Growing demand, limited hiring. Demand for content grows and hiring is limited. The programme lets the team absorb the work with 10.0 Speculative hires in place of 79.6 Modelled. Those avoided hires count only if they were planned and funded.

Scenario C still costs more than today. Spend after absorbing the demand is $11,912,448 Speculative a year above today's spend, so in cash against today there is no saving and no payback.     Why doubled capacity is not doubled revenue

    Two peer-reviewed studies set the limits of what a CMO should expect, and a third body of evidence is the page's own: more output is not more value if it adds waste.

    Shapiro, Hitsch and Tuchman (July 2021) estimated the return on television advertising for a large panel of consumer brands. They found much smaller advertising elasticities than earlier work, and negative returns at the margin for more than 80% Benchmark of brands. The study covers television advertising for consumer goods, not content production, so it shows that the marginal unit of exposure often earns little, not that marginal assets do.

    Lewis and Rao (6 July 2015) ran a series of large field experiments in digital advertising with major retailers and brokerages. The median confidence interval on return was over 100 percentage points Benchmark wide. Their finding is about measurement, not about size: the return to advertising is hard to measure, and selection bias makes observational estimates unreliable. That is why this page asks for holdouts.

    A third caution is internal. Output beyond what is used is waste, and output beyond what a reviewer or a test can read is noise. The experiments table in the first edition's levers shows the same limit for tests: more tests on the same traffic do not give more conclusive wins.

    Four mechanisms are defensible, because each can be measured separately from volume.

    Speed to market
    Launching in additional markets sooner captures demand that would otherwise go unserved. It is measured per market, against the market that launched on the old schedule.
    Learning velocity
    More well-powered tests and faster replacement of tired creative. It is measured by conclusive tests per quarter, not tests started.
    Reallocation
    Hours and agency spend move to work with a higher marginal return. It needs marginal-return curves from a marketing-mix model, and it is where released capacity earns its keep.
    Market coverage
    Markets, segments and long-tail pages that were never served, where response is not yet saturated. This is the most plausible source of new demand, and the slice in the smallest useful version is built around it.

    None of the four is claimed as a result. Each is a place for a holdout to look.

    How lift would be proven

    Output is easy to count and easy to inflate. An outcome claim therefore needs a design that could show it to be wrong. These four steps, in this order, are the minimum.

    1. Holdouts. Keep a comparable audience, market or channel on the old way of working, and treat the difference against it as the only evidence of lift. Before and after comparisons confuse the programme with the season.
    2. Pre-registered metrics. Write down the outcome metric, the guardrails and the stopping rule before launch. Choosing the measure after seeing the data lets any programme look like a success.
    3. Multiple-comparison correction. A large variant pool multiplies tests, and every extra test multiplies the chance of a false winner. Correct for the number of comparisons, or the extra volume will produce extra noise that looks like lift.
    4. MMM calibrated by incrementality tests. A marketing-mix model can estimate the effect across channels, but only if geographic or holdout experiments anchor it. The method comes from the other five levers, where the insight lever treats model refresh as an output. Modelled

    Risks and assumptions

    Software built → Autonomy enabled → Human work reduced → Marketing capacity increased → Business outcomes improved

    Every link in the chain can fail, and agents that act add risks that a content tool does not have. This section first sets out the risks that come with agents, each with a mitigation or a test, and then keeps the first edition's account of what goes wrong with AI-made marketing content.

    Risks that come with agents

    These are design risks, not incidents. Each names the way the programme could mislead its owners or harm the brand, and what would catch it.

    Irreversible publication

    An agent that can publish can publish a mistake. A page that went live, an email that was sent or a budget that was spent cannot be called back by a rerun.

    Mitigation: Stage, verify, then publish. Agents prepare and a deterministic check verifies before anything goes live, publishing and spend are capped per action, and a kill switch stops every agent at once. Permission rises only for action classes with audit evidence.

    Contested ground truth

    Brand, legal and regulatory judgement is not always settled. If reviewers disagree, the set the agents are tested against is itself uncertain, and a high score proves little.

    Mitigation: Give every rule an identifier and an owner, record disagreements instead of averaging them away, and make legal the owner of any new claim. A rule with no agreed answer stays with a person.

    Compounding multi-step error

    A run that must get every one of many steps right succeeds far less often than any single step does. Small per-step error rates multiply across a long workflow, so a chain that looks reliable step by step can fail often end to end.

    Test: Measure per-step success and end-to-end success separately in the pilot, and price escalation to a person from the measured per-step figures, not from an assumed one. A step whose success cannot be measured is not automated.

    Judge drift

    A review agent that scores other agents' work can drift as its model, its prompts or the work itself change. A newer model is not always a better judge: in the one public reference, a newer model tested worse than its predecessor.

    Test: Track the agreement between the review agent and a human-curated set for each tier and each rule, and rerun it before any change. Recalibration is a recurring human cost, and it belongs in the platform team's budget.

    Inference and token spend

    Agents that loop, retry and call other agents can spend without limit. At current prices the model shows inference at $9,609 Speculative a year, which is small. That can change with price, with longer chains or with a fault.

    Mitigation: Cap spend per run and per day, monitor it against the run record, and review it with the platform team each quarter. Treat a sudden rise as an incident.

    Goodhart on the autonomy percentage

    The share of work completed autonomously is easy to raise by narrowing what counts as eligible or by loosening review. When it becomes a target, it stops measuring autonomy.

    Mitigation: Never report it alone. Pair it with the escape rate, first-pass acceptance and the share of actions that achieved their verified objective, and freeze the eligibility definition before the pilot starts.

    Labour relations

    Role change handled badly costs trust, and the capacity the programme depends on is the people who must use it. Removing capacity is a separate decision from releasing it, and it carries its own cost: severance in scenario B is priced at $2,926,347 Speculative.

    Mitigation: Consult employee representatives and works councils early, let any removal follow measured adoption, show released hours as redeployed before anything is called a saving, and keep severance on its own line.

    Model regressions

    A model update can change behaviour without notice, and an agent that passed its tests last month may not pass them this month.

    Mitigation: Pin model versions, rerun the evaluation sets before every upgrade, and keep the ability to roll back. Autonomy that was earned on one model version is re-earned on the next.

    Volume without variety produces sameness, and audiences notice. The risk is a library of correct assets that all look and read alike.

    The factory model in full

    The first workflow section keeps the principle: derive, do not generate. This is the model behind it, drawn as a factory with inputs, stages and a gate.

    Think of the marketing organisation as a factory rather than a studio. A factory has inputs, stages and queues, and it is judged on throughput, defect rate and cost per good unit. The inputs here are briefs and a small set of master concepts. The stages are derivation, review and publishing. Queues form wherever one stage produces faster than the next can absorb. Seen this way, doubling output is a question about the slowest stage, not about how quickly anyone can draft copy.

    The principle is to derive, not generate. A few master concepts carry the creative risk: the idea, the claim, the look and the offer. People direct and approve them, and that is the slow, expensive part by design. Most of the variants that follow are mechanical derivations: a template applied, a locale swapped, a feed rendered at the sizes a channel demands. A derivation changes the form of an approved idea; it does not invent a new one. That is how variants multiply without multiplying risk.

Once derivation is automated, reviewer capacity sets the pace, and a programme that ignores this only builds a longer queue in front of the same few people. That is why review is drawn as the gate, and why the auto-approval gate is treated in detail later.

    B

    Appendix B: The review agent

    Supports: What needs to be built

    An independent review agent checks every variant against the versioned policy before any approval, and records what it found and what it could not check.

    The agent follows a short set of rules. They are what make its verdict auditable rather than a bare yes or no.

    • Cites the rule ID. Every finding names the policy rule it enforces, so a person can read why an asset failed.
    • Writes to the asset manifest. Each finding is stored against the asset, with the policy version and a link to the log.
    • Runs a bounded fix loop. A failing asset gets a limited number of repair attempts, then the agent stops and escalates.
    • Can escalate, never lower. The agent may raise an asset to a higher tier, but it can never lower one; only policy assigns tiers.
    • Never trusts the generator's self-report. It re-checks from the asset itself, not from what the generator says about it.
    • Records what it could not check. An unchecked item is a finding, and unchecked means escalate, not pass.
    • Uses a different model. The reviewer runs on a different model or prompt family from the generator, so the two do not share blind spots.
    • Separates eligibility from quality. Eligibility, meaning which tier an asset sits in, is a policy question; quality is a judgement question. Approval needs both.

    In practice a verdict is a short record: pass, fix or escalate, each with the rule IDs behind it. A pass with nothing unchecked can proceed to the tier gate. A fix returns to the generator with the cited rules as instructions. An escalation goes to a person with the findings attached, so the person starts from the evidence and not from the asset alone. How often the agent itself is right is measured by the eval harness.

    The review loop is a pattern, not an invention. This is the equivalent of Intercom's review agent, which inspired the design described here. Modelled

    Guarding against reviewer fatigue

    • Blind-first review. The reviewer decides before seeing the agent's verdict, so agreement is evidence and not anchoring.
    • Seeded-error catch rate. Known-bad assets are mixed into queues; a falling catch rate is the standing fatigue signal.
    • Session caps and queue rotation. Review sessions are limited and reviewers move between asset classes.
    • Random sampling of auto-approvals. A person audits a random slice of what the gate cleared, so the gate is never trusted unchecked.
    • Drift alarms. Sudden shifts in approval rate or time per asset trigger a look before quality slips.
    • Mandatory reason codes. Every override says why, which feeds the eval harness.

    Appendix D: Eval harness and telemetry

    Supports: What needs to be built

    The eval harness is how the organisation proves the gate is safe, and keeps proving it as models, prompts and policy change.

    Ground truth is the set of decisions that reviewers have already made. Every human decision carries a reason code, so an override with a reason code is the cheapest ground-truth feed there is: it costs the reviewer one extra click and gives the harness a labelled example. Datasets are versioned, so a result can always be traced to the data that produced it.

    Agreement is reported per criterion and per tier, never as one average. Rights and claims will be the hard criteria, and an average would hide a weak spot behind strong results on resizes and crops. The harness also feeds in seeded assets that are known to be bad, including rights traps, and samples auto-approvals at random, so it can estimate how often the gate lets through something a person would have stopped.

    A harness can be gamed by the thing it evaluates. If the agent or its authors can see the seeded assets, they will learn them. Keep the seeded and held-out sets apart from anything used to tune prompts, rotate them, and let someone who does not own the generator maintain them.

    Expect most changes to be unmeasurable. In the one public reference, most tested changes were inconclusive and a newer model tested worse than its predecessor. The harness therefore compares a change with a baseline and ships it only if it holds inside the noise band. Thresholds are relative on purpose: they must be calibrated on the organisation's own data, and this page does not invent a benchmark. Modelled

    No change to a generator, prompt, model or policy ships if it regresses the harness.

    Telemetry

    Telemetry joins the asset ID in the manifest to ad-platform and CRM performance, so each asset's policy version, review path, cost and outcome sit on one record. That join is what lets this page's cost and quality figures be measured and not only modelled.

    • First-pass compliance: the share of variants that clear review without rework.
    • Auto-approval share by tier.
    • Reviewer utilisation against capacity.
    • Queue age: how long the oldest items have waited.
    • Rework rate.
    • Cost per approved asset.
    • Brief-to-live time.
    • Policy and skill version in use for each asset.

    This is instrumentation of the work, not surveillance of people: metrics are per asset, per tier and per queue, never per named reviewer. Modelled

    What this evidence implies

    • The suites are converging on this design. Each documents orchestrators, brand context, review agents, approvals and goal-driven campaign agents. The spine on this page is not a rival idea; it is the common shape, described in terms you can test.
    • Each works inside its own walls.     • The choice depends on your stack. In a single-suite organisation, buy the orchestration and own the rules, evals, ledger and measures. In a multi-vendor organisation, which is the common enterprise case, the thin layer across systems is the part to build.
    • Do not duplicate in-channel optimisation. Ad platforms already automate audience, placement and budget. The system's job is to feed them approved creative and objectives, set caps, and measure with holdouts.

    Build, buy and lock-in

    • Exports and data ownership. Require export of content, review decisions and logs by file or API, and keep a copy in storage you control.     • Keep the policy source outside any one tool. Tier rules, claims rules and rights rules live in versioned skills described under platform and skills, and tools read them rather than hold the only copy.
